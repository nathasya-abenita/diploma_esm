# Slide 8: Physical mechanisms

1. Introduction (15 Seconds) "Figure 5 summarizes why short-duration rainfall often intensifies faster than the Clausius–Clapeyron (CC) rate of 7% per degree. It visualizes the feedbacks across three scales that either invigorate or suppress a storm."
2. The 100 m Scale: The Convective Core (40 Seconds) "At the smallest scale—100 meters—we see the heart of the convective process.

- Updrafts and Depth: Warming moisture fuels stronger updrafts and increases convection depth, allowing clouds to grow taller.
- Microphysics: A higher melting level increases the liquid water content in the cloud, enhancing surface rain.
- Entrainment: However, mixing drier air into the core—entrainment—acts as a negative feedback that can suppress weaker storms. This scale is the most difficult to model, requiring the Large-eddy simulations we discussed in Lecture 7."

3. The 1 km Scale: Storm Footprint & Organization (40 Seconds) "Moving to the 1 kilometer scale, we see the storm's impact on its immediate area.

- Latent Heating: The condensation of extra moisture releases latent heat, providing a massive energy boost to the system—a core concept from Lectures 2 and 6.
- Cold Pools: Evaporating rain creates cold pools that spread out, triggering new storm cells and increasing the rainfall area.
- Heavy Precipitation: The combination of these factors results in the intensified heavy precipitation we observe in Convection-Permitting Models (CPMs)."

4. The 100+ km Scale: The Large-Scale Environment (25 Seconds) "Finally, at the 100+ kilometer synoptic scale, the environment acts as a regulator.

- Stability: The upper troposphere warms faster than the surface, increasing tropospheric stability, which can act as a 'brake' on storm formation.
- Inflow: To sustain super-CC rates, storms must draw in moisture from this wider environment through large-scale moisture convergence."

# Slide 9: Implications for flood hazards

1. Introduction
"Having discussed the atmospheric physics, we must now look at the surface impacts. Figure 6 summarizes how these intensified extremes translate into flash flood hazards, which are defined as floods occurring in less than 90 minutes. The critical takeaway here is that the flood response is not linear; a small increase in rainfall intensity can lead to a disproportionately large increase in flood severity."

2. The Mechanism of Non-Linearity
"This non-linearity is driven by two main factors:

- Excess Rainfall: As rainfall intensity increases, we reach a threshold where vegetation and soil can no longer absorb moisture. Proportionally more water is converted into 'excess rainfall' that flows directly into drainage systems.
- Flow Velocity: Once this water enters the catchment, flood peaks increase non-linearly because higher water discharge leads to increased flow velocities, compounding the impact and momentum of the flood."

3. The Urban 'Amplifier' 
"Urban areas are particularly vulnerable flash-flood hotspots due to a compounding loop shown in the center of Figure 6.

- First, the Urban Heat Island effect destabilizes the local atmosphere, which can further invigorate convective storms.
- Second, cities are dominated by impermeable surfaces. Our aging drainage infrastructure was designed for historical rainfall rates and is often overwhelmed by these anthropogenic intensifications, leading to severe socio-economic damage."

The Urban Amplifier
"Finally, we must consider the surface environment. In our Earth System Modelling course, we discussed the unique energy and water balance of cities. Urbanization compounds this flood risk by replacing natural absorbers with impermeable surfaces. This ensures that almost all of that intensified 'anthropogenic rainfall' is converted directly into flash floods, overwhelming infrastructure designed for a cooler, drier past
